## Structure of Critique

There are many different ways to conduct a critique. As a class, we can decide what works best for giving and receiving feedback.

The most important part of critique is **being present**. Laptops, phones, and other electronic devices should be put away for the **entire duration of a critique**.

Good critique is foundational to pushing a project further. The way we will conduct critique in this class will be both **verbal and written**.

At the end of each student's presentation, I will first ask if there is anything specific that **you, as the artist**, would like feedback on: an area of uncertainty, a technical or conceptual question, an implementation problem, or something you need an extra set of eyes and brains on.

Then we will move on to class criticism.

### Giving Criticism

Critique is generally most useful when it is **specific**. To help guide your responses, you can use this framework, adapted from *Varieties of Visual Experience* by Edmund Burke:

1. **Description:** What are you seeing? What details stand out?

2. **Analysis:** How is it made? What formal, technical, material, or compositional decisions are you noticing? What does the work make you think about or feel? What does it remind you of?

3. **Interpretation:** What do you think the work is about? What ideas, questions, or relationships does it seem to be exploring?

4. **Evaluation:** Is the project successful in communicating or exploring those ideas? Does it approach the prompt in a compelling, interesting, or unique way? What is working particularly well, and where might there be opportunities to push the work further?

**Avoid simply saying "I like it."**

It's completely fine to like something. The important part is explaining **why**. Pick up on a specific detail, observe it, and explain why you noticed it or why it stood out to you.

You might say:

> "I keep coming back to the way the image is cropped because it makes the space feel much more enclosed than I expected."

Or:

> "The repetition of these forms makes me think about industrial production, but I'm not sure if that is intentional."

Specific observations give the artist something to work with.

### Receiving Criticism

Understanding yourself and your own relationship to your work is an important part of developing the ability to receive criticism.

Critical feedback is not necessarily negative. Critique brings together several different minds and experiences in order to push a project further. An important skill is learning to separate **yourself as an individual from the identity of your work**.

Receiving criticism can also open up ways of understanding your work that you may not have considered yourself. Everyone is coming from a different set of experiences, references, and assumptions, so people may understand your project differently than you intended.

Some ways of receiving criticism, adapted from *The Artist's Way* by Julia Cameron:

* **Jot down notes for yourself.** What concepts or comments feel compelling? What bothers you? What do you want to come back to later?

* **Don't treat one project as a final judgment of your ability.** A work that doesn't fully succeed may still be an important stepping stone toward the next work.

* **Look for patterns.** Has this criticism come up in previous projects? If so, is there something worth reconsidering?

* **Give yourself time.** You don't have to immediately agree with or act on every piece of feedback. Some criticism may become useful later.

* **Make something.** One way to respond to criticism is to build something new, revise the existing work, or use the criticism as a starting point for another experiment.
